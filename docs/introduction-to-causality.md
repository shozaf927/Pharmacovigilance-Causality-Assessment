# Introduction to Causality Assessment

## 1. What is causality assessment?
Causality assessment is a structured evaluation of the likelihood that a medicinal product contributed to an observed adverse event. It supports individual case review and pharmacovigilance documentation; it does not usually prove causation with certainty.

## 2. Adverse event versus adverse drug reaction
An **adverse event (AE)** is an untoward medical occurrence after exposure to a medicine, without necessarily having a causal relationship. An **adverse drug reaction (ADR)** implies at least a reasonable possibility of a causal relationship.

Temporal sequence alone is insufficient: an event occurring after treatment may be due to the underlying disease, another medicine, an infection, an unrelated condition or coincidence.

## 3. Why assess causality?
- Support consistent and transparent case evaluation.
- Identify missing clinical information and alternative explanations.
- Inform signal detection and benefit–risk evaluation.
- Support appropriate follow-up and regulatory documentation.

## 4. Core principles
1. Establish a reliable timeline.
2. Verify the suspected product, dose, route and exposure dates.
3. Characterise the reaction with clinical findings.
4. Evaluate patient factors and alternative causes.
5. Review dechallenge and any naturally occurring rechallenge information.
6. Apply a recognised method without treating the score as proof.
7. Document uncertainty and missing data.

## 5. Individual assessment versus population-level evidence
Individual case assessment asks whether a medicine plausibly caused a particular patient's event. Epidemiological studies assess associations across populations and may quantify risks. Both are important, but their methods and conclusions are not interchangeable.

## 6. Spontaneous reports
Spontaneous reporting systems collect suspected ADRs. Reports are valuable for signal detection even when causality is uncertain. A report should not be rejected solely because the reporter cannot prove causation; suspected relationships should be documented and assessed according to applicable reporting rules.

## 7. Limitations
- Incomplete or retrospective clinical information.
- Confounding by indication and underlying disease.
- Polypharmacy and drug interactions.
- Lack of specific diagnostic tests for many ADRs.
- Subjectivity and inter-rater variation.
- Rechallenge is often unavailable or unethical.

**Takeaway:** A causality category is a reasoned assessment of available evidence at a particular time, not an immutable verdict.
