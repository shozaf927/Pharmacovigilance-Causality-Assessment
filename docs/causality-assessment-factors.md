# Causality Assessment Factors

Assess four broad groups of evidence. Record facts separately from interpretation.

## 1. Drug-related factors
- Generic and brand name; manufacturer/batch when relevant.
- Dose, route, frequency, formulation and duration.
- Indication and reason for treatment.
- Known reaction in approved product information or reliable literature.
- Pharmacological mechanism and dose plausibility.
- Medication errors, overdose, interactions and concomitant medicines.
- Drug concentrations or relevant pharmacokinetic information, if available.

## 2. Event-related factors
- Precise clinical description and diagnosis.
- Date/time of onset, duration and progression.
- Objective findings: examination, laboratory results, imaging or other tests.
- Known phenotype and latency for the suspected reaction.
- Whether the event is compatible with the product's known safety profile.

## 3. Patient-related factors
- Age and relevant physiological characteristics.
- Medical history, comorbidities and allergies.
- Renal and hepatic function.
- Genetic or metabolic susceptibility when supported by evidence.
- Pregnancy status where relevant.
- Underlying disease and its natural course.

## 4. Clinical-course-related factors
- Time from first dose or dose change to event onset.
- Dechallenge: whether the event improved after dose reduction or withdrawal.
- Rechallenge: whether the event recurred after re-exposure, if this occurred naturally or for a clinical reason.
- Treatment given for the reaction and response.
- Alternative explanations, including disease progression, infection and other medicines.

## 5. Timeline worksheet
| Milestone | Information to capture |
|---|---|
| Baseline | Patient condition before exposure |
| Start | First dose and indication |
| Change | Dose/route/formulation changes |
| Onset | First symptoms/signs and objective evidence |
| Intervention | Withdrawal, dose reduction or treatment |
| Follow-up | Improvement, persistence, recurrence or sequelae |

## 6. Interpretation cautions
- A known labelled reaction supports plausibility but does not prove the individual case.
- A positive dechallenge supports causality but may be confounded by treatment of the event or natural recovery.
- A negative dechallenge does not automatically exclude causality; some reactions persist.
- Do not deliberately rechallenge where it could expose the patient to harm.
- Lack of information is not equivalent to evidence against causality.
